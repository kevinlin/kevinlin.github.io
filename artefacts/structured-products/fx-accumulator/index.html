FX Accumulator and TARF

An accumulator is a contract to buy a currency every week at a rate better than today's. It ends early if the currency rises, and it makes the investor buy double if the currency falls. A target redemption forward works the same way and ends once the investor's gains reach a set total.

A good rate, on terms that favour the bank

The investor agrees to buy a fixed amount of one currency against another on a schedule, weekly or monthly, for up to a year. The price is the strike rate, set below the market rate on the trade date. No premium is paid at the start. The investor posts margin, and every period the two currencies change hands.

Two features pay for the favourable strike. The first is the knock-out. If the market reaches the knock-out rate, the contract ends and nothing more is bought, so a rally stops the gains. The second is gearing. On any fixing where the market is below the strike, the investor buys twice the amount, still at the strike. The contract never ends because of losses.

So the investor's gains are limited and counted once, and the losses run for the full tenor at double size. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority tells banks not to describe an accumulator as a way to buy an asset at a discount, and to give it the highest risk rating.

In DBS's booklet the investor can lose up to the maximum notional amount, which is the periodic amount times the gearing times the number of fixings. That is far more than the margin posted.

At or above the knock-outThe contract endsOnly what was settled before the knock-out counts. No more purchases.
At or above the strikeBuy 1× at the strikeThe market is higher than the price paid, so this fixing shows a gain.
Below the strikeBuy 2× at the strikeThe market is lower than the price paid, on twice the amount.

Every fixing

What is inside

The HKMA describes an accumulator as a series of options or forward contracts in one contract, written by the customer. The split below is the usual way to read each fixing and is not taken from a term sheet.

Piece, per fixingInvestor's sideWhat it does
Call at the strikeBought, 1×Above the strike the investor buys at the strike and gains the difference.
Put at the strikeSold, 2×Below the strike the investor must buy twice the amount at the strike.
Knock-outGiven to the bankCancels every remaining call and put once the market reaches the knock-out rate. The calls are the valuable ones at that point.

The investor sells two puts for each call bought, and accepts a knock-out that removes the calls when they are worth most. The value of all that is spent on one thing: moving the strike below the forward rate. No cash changes hands at the start because the pieces are priced to cancel out, with the bank's margin inside.

This also explains the mark-to-market. DBS warns that the contract can lose value when implied volatility rises, even if the exchange rate has not moved. The investor is a net seller of options, and options cost more when volatility is higher.

TARF: a knock-out on total gains

A target redemption forward, or target accumulator, has no knock-out rate. It ends when the investor's gains, added up fixing by fixing, reach the target. Losing fixings do not count towards the target and do not end anything.

Take fixings of 83.00, 83.10 and 83.05. The first counts 0.30 × 1,000,000 = INR 300,000. The second adds 200,000. The third would add 250,000 and take the total past the cap, so the client is paid only the remaining 100,000 and the other nine fixings are cancelled. The client's best case over the whole year is INR 600,000.

Now take a rupee that weakens to 86 and stays there. Every month the client sells USD 2,000,000 at 83.30 when the market pays 86.00. That is INR 5,400,000 a month given up, for twelve months, with no target in sight.

Minimum accumulation. Some knock-out accumulators guarantee that a minimum amount is exchanged even if the knock-out happens right after the trade date. It gives the investor a few sure fixings at the strike.

Margin

The investor does not pay the notional up front. DBS asks for an initial margin of 10% of the remaining maximum notional, plus the whole of any mark-to-market loss.

The last row is the one to remember. The bank sets the margin percentage and may change it, and it sets the mark-to-market value with its own pricing model. A margin call arrives at short notice, and positions can be closed out at a loss if it is not met.

The HKMA requires banks to sell accumulators only to professional investors, to measure the customer's exposure on the full notional amount, and to offer simpler alternatives with the reason for choosing the accumulator on record.
