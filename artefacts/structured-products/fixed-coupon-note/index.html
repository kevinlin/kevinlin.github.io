Fixed Coupon Note

A fixed coupon note pays the same coupon every month whatever the shares do, and puts the client's capital at risk in exchange. This page follows one note from price request to redemption and names every field a placement flow has to carry along the way.

What the client is buying

The client lends money to an issuer for a few months. Inside the same note, the client also agrees to buy a share at the strike price if things go badly. The coupon is the payment for taking that risk, which is why it is much higher than a deposit rate. The best case is par plus coupons. The worst case is owning a share that has fallen a long way, bought at the strike.

Most notes are linked to two or three shares at once, and every test looks at the worst performer of the basket. Most also carry two barriers. The knock-out barrier ends the trade early when the shares are doing well. The knock-in barrier decides whether a fall in the shares can reach the client's capital at all.

You will see the product called FCN, fixed coupon ELN or FCELN in Asia, and an autocallable barrier reverse convertible in Europe. The structure is the same and the local habits differ.

Outcome 1Knocked out earlyOn an observation date every share closes at or above the knock-out barrier. The client gets par back plus the coupons so far, and the trade is over.
Outcome 2Redeemed at par at maturityNo knock-out. At final valuation the worst performer is at or above the strike, or the knock-in barrier was never breached. Par plus every coupon.
Outcome 3Converted into sharesNo knock-out, the knock-in happened, and the worst performer finishes below the strike. The client receives that share, bought at the strike price, plus every coupon.

    What moves the coupon

    The coupon is the price of the risk the client takes on. Anything that makes a loss more likely, or the note longer-lived, lets the issuer pay more. This is why the pricing screen feels like a set of levers.

    ChangeCouponWhy
    More volatile sharesHigherA bigger chance of finishing below the strike.
    More shares in the basketHigherOnly one of them has to fall for the client to lose.
    Shares that move less alikeHigherWith low correlation the worst performer tends to end further from the rest.
    Lower strikeLowerThe client buys the share cheaper, so the loss starts later and is smaller.
    Lower knock-in barrierLowerThe shares have to fall further before capital is exposed.
    European knock-in in place of AmericanLowerOnly the last day counts, so a dip in the middle of the life is forgiven.
    No knock-in barrierHigherAny finish below the strike converts into shares.
    Higher knock-out barrierHigherThe note is harder to call, stays alive longer and carries the downside for longer.
    Higher dividends on the sharesHigherThe client does not receive them. The issuer pays part of their value back as coupon.
    Higher interest rates, or an issuer that pays more to borrowHigherThe note is also a loan to the issuer. A weaker issuer pays more, and the client takes more credit risk.
    Higher distributor feeLowerThe fee comes out of the same pot as the coupon.
