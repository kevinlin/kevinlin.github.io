Bonus Enhanced Note

A BEN pays nothing during its life. At maturity it pays a fixed bonus if the worst share of a basket is at or above the strike price, and more than the bonus if that share has risen further. Below the strike the bonus is gone and the investor owns the share.

One day decides, and the strike is a cliff

The investor pays 100% of the notional and receives no coupon while the note is alive. Everything is settled from the closing prices on the final valuation date. With a basket, only the worst performer counts: the share with the lowest final price relative to its initial price.

If the worst performer closes at or above its strike price, the investor receives the notional plus the higher of two amounts: the bonus coupon, or the rise of the worst performer since the trade date. The gain has no upper limit. If the worst performer closes below its strike price, there is no bonus, and the investor receives that share at the strike price.

The step at the strike price is what sets the BEN apart. With a 20% bonus and a strike at 80%, a close exactly at the strike returns 120%. One tick lower returns shares worth just under 100%. Near maturity, a small move in one share can be worth the whole bonus.

Julius Baer describes the BEN as a recovery trade for an investor who is neutral or mildly positive on the shares, and as an alternative to buying them outright.

Outcome 1UpsideThe worst performer has risen by more than the bonus. The investor receives the notional plus that rise.
Outcome 2BonusThe worst performer is at or above the strike but has not risen by more than the bonus. Notional plus the bonus coupon.
Outcome 3Shares, no bonusThe worst performer is below the strike. The investor receives notional ÷ strike price shares of it, or their cash value.

Formula from the uSMART guide. At or above the strike: notional × (100% + the higher of the bonus coupon and worst performer final ÷ initial − 100%). Below the strike: notional ÷ strike price in shares, or notional × final ÷ strike in cash.

The investor is paid for the put and gives up all interest on the deposit. Both go into the digital and the call. An FCN spends the same two sources on a coupon that arrives every month. A BEN spends them on one large payment that depends on the last day.

A basket raises the bonus for the same reason it raises an FCN coupon. The put is on whichever share turns out worst, so it is worth more.

BEN and FCN on the same shares

Both notes have the investor sell a put at the strike on the worst performer. They differ in how the investor is paid for it.

Variant with a knock-in barrier, from Julius Baer. Below the strike, the investor still gets the notional back in cash as long as the barrier was never breached, but no bonus. Shares are delivered only when the barrier was breached and the share ends below the strike. Julius Baer's example: strike at 100, bonus 30%, American knock-in at 70.
