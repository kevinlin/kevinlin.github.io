Foreign Exchange

Every FX product is built from four contracts: spot, forward, swap and option. This page covers how a rate is quoted, why the forward rate differs from the spot rate, and what an option pays. The currency-linked investment and the accumulator on the next pages are combinations of these.

A rate is the price of the first currency

A currency pair is written with two codes. EUR/USD 1.1000 means one euro costs 1.1000 US dollars. The first currency is the base currency, the thing being priced. The second is the quote currency, the money it is priced in. When the number goes up, the base currency has become more expensive.

Every statement about a rate depends on that order. A structured product that pays out "if the rate is above the strike" means the base currency is strong. Read the pair first, then the level.

A dealer quotes two rates: a bid, at which the dealer buys the base currency, and a higher offer, at which the dealer sells it. The gap is the spread, and it is how a customer pays for the trade. Most pairs are quoted to four decimal places, and the fourth is called a pip. Pairs against the yen are quoted to two.

The amounts on the two sides are linked by the rate. Buying EUR 1,000,000 at 1.1000 means paying USD 1,100,000. If the rate then moves to 1.1100, the euros are worth USD 10,000 more: 100 pips on one million.

Workbench: the forward rate and the option

EUR/USD on EUR 1,000,000. The rates are made up for the example. Change the two interest rates and watch which way the forward moves. Then look at one option from the buyer's side and from the seller's.

The forward rate is arithmetic and holds no forecast. Someone who needs dollars in three months can agree a forward today. Or they can sell euros for dollars at spot and put the dollars on deposit. Both routes must cost the same, or dealers would take the difference. So the forward is the spot rate adjusted for the interest earned on each currency over the period.

The currency with the lower interest rate is worth more in the forward market than at spot. Holding it earns less, and the forward price makes up for that. The BIS calls this relation, covered interest parity, the closest thing to a physical law in international finance. It also reports that since 2008 the forward has drifted from the formula by a small amount called the cross-currency basis. For learning the products, the formula is close enough.

Formula from the BIS article, for a period shorter than a year. Forward = spot × (1 + quote currency rate × days ÷ 360) ÷ (1 + base currency rate × days ÷ 360). Forward points = forward − spot, quoted in pips.

What gets built from them

The structured products on the next pages contain nothing new. Each one packages forwards and options so that the customer sells some optionality and is paid for it in a better rate or a higher yield.

ProductMade ofThe customer is paid withThe customer gives up
Currency-Linked InvestmentA deposit and one sold optionInterest well above the deposit rateThe choice of currency at maturity
AccumulatorA strip of bought calls and twice as many sold puts, with a knock-outA strike better than the forward rateGains after a rally, and double losses after a fall
TARFThe same strip, ended by a profit targetA strike better than the forward rateAll gains beyond the target

The seller's view of the option in the workbench is the one to keep in mind. In every product above the customer is on that side: a small sure amount, and a loss that grows with the move.
