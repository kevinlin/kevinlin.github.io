Currency-Linked Investment

A CLI is a short deposit that pays far more interest than a time deposit. In return, the bank may repay it in another currency at an exchange rate fixed on day one. That happens when the other currency has fallen below that rate, so the investor receives it at a price above the market.

Two currencies, one rate, one day

The investor picks three things when placing the money: the deposit currency, a linked currency, and a tenor that is usually between one week and a few months. The investor and the bank then agree a conversion rate between the two currencies. Everything else follows from the market rate at one moment on the fixing date.

If the linked currency is at or above the conversion rate at the fixing, the investor gets the principal and the interest back in the deposit currency. If it is below, the bank converts the principal and the interest into the linked currency at the conversion rate and pays that. The amount is fixed, but each unit of it is now worth less than the bank charged for it.

The investor is paid the high interest for agreeing to this. The Hong Kong investor education body IFEC describes it as selling a put option on the linked currency to the bank. It is the currency version of the Equity-Linked Note: the linked currency plays the share, and the conversion rate plays the strike price.

People use it in two ways. Some want the interest and expect the linked currency to hold. Others want to buy the linked currency anyway and set the conversion rate at the price they would be happy to pay. This second use is common practice and is not taken from the sources below.

Outcome 1Deposit currency backThe linked currency fixes at or above the conversion rate. Principal plus interest, in the currency that was deposited.
Outcome 2Linked currency deliveredThe linked currency fixes below the conversion rate. Principal plus interest, converted at the conversion rate and paid in the linked currency.

Formula from the IFEC example. Interest = principal × yearly rate × days ÷ 365 for a Hong Kong dollar deposit. At or above the conversion rate: principal + interest. Below it: (principal + interest) ÷ conversion rate, in the linked currency.

The IFEC example: HKD 60,000 for 14 days at 16.5% a year, linked to the Australian dollar, conversion rate 6.9588 Hong Kong dollars per Australian dollar. The two buttons load IFEC's scenarios. What is inside

A time deposit and one sold option. IFEC names both pieces. The split of the interest between them is arithmetic on the payoff and is not taken from a term sheet.

PieceInvestor's sideWhat it does at maturityIn the IFEC example
Time depositLends to the bankRepays the principal with ordinary deposit interest.HKD 60,000 for 14 days
Put on the linked currencySoldBelow the conversion rate, the bank uses it to hand over the linked currency at the conversion rate.At 6.2300 the bank pays in AUD at 6.9588
Option premiumReceivedPaid to the investor as extra interest. It is most of the quoted rate.Part of the 16.5% a year, HKD 379.73 in all

The quoted rate is a yearly rate on a deposit of days. 16.5% a year for 14 days is 0.63% of the principal. The loss in IFEC's second scenario is 9.9% of the principal. The interest is small and certain, and the loss is rare and large. Every product that sells an option has this shape.

Three things raise the rate the bank can offer, because each makes the sold put worth more: a conversion rate closer to the current market rate, a currency pair that moves more, and a longer tenor.

The capital protected form

Banks also sell a version that always repays the principal in the deposit currency. HSBC calls its one Capital Protected Investment, Currency Linked III. The exchange rate decides only which of two interest amounts is paid.

HSBC's exampleValue
DepositGBP 100,000 for 6 months, linked to USD
Spot rate on the trade dateGBP/USD 1.3190
Trigger rate1.3390, which is the spot rate plus 0.02
Fixing at or above the trigger100.37% of the principal: GBP 100,370
Fixing below the trigger100.28% of the principal: GBP 100,280

Here the investor buys an option with part of the deposit interest and sells nothing. The cost of protection shows in the numbers. The whole bet is worth GBP 90 on GBP 100,000 over six months.

Principal protection covers the exchange rate and nothing else. HSBC's own worst case is the bank failing, where the investor is an unsecured creditor. And an investor whose home currency is the Hong Kong dollar still carries the pound: in HSBC's side example the pound falls from 10.31 to 10.10 against the Hong Kong dollar, and the position is down HKD 18,172, or 1.76%, after the interest.

What can go wrong

From the IFEC page and HSBC's risk disclosure.

Exchange rateRepaid in a fallen currencyThe loss grows with the fall and has no floor. IFEC's wording is that the investor could lose all the money in the worst case.
Capped gainInterest is the most it paysIf the linked currency rallies, the investor earns the interest and none of the rally.
The bankUnsecured creditorNo collateral and no deposit protection. If the bank fails, the investor queues with its other creditors.
LiquidityHeld to maturityEarly redemption is not allowed in the IFEC example. HSBC may agree to it case by case, at a price it sets.
