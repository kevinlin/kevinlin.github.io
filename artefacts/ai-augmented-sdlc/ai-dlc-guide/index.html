User guide · AWS method

The AI-Driven Development Lifecycle

AI-DLC is AWS's method for building software with AI at the centre of every step. AI writes the plan, asks what it doesn't know and does the work. People answer, correct and approve. This guide covers the ideas behind the method, its vocabulary, its three phases, and prompts you can try today.

Based on the AWS DevOps blog announcement (July 2025) and the AI-DLC method definition paper by Raja SP, Amazon Web Services.

  • 3phases: Inception, Construction, Operations
  • 2team rituals: Mob Elaboration, Mob Construction
  • Hours–daysper Bolt, instead of multi-week sprints
  • 1 dayfor a practitioner to start using it

01 · Context

Why AI-DLC exists

Scrum and most SDLC methods were designed around people doing long-running work. Product owners, architects and developers spend much of their week on planning, meetings and other rituals. AWS argues that adding an AI assistant to that process keeps those costs in place and limits what the AI can do.

Organisations mostly use AI in one of two ways today. In AI-assisted development, AI speeds up individual tasks such as code completion, documentation or tests. In AI-autonomous development, AI is expected to produce a whole application from requirements with no human involvement. AWS reports that both give disappointing results for speed and quality.

The method rests on two ideas:

  • AI-powered execution with human oversight. AI builds detailed work plans, asks for clarification, and leaves critical decisions to people, who hold the business context.
  • Dynamic team collaboration. Because AI takes on the routine work, the team spends more of its time together, solving problems and making decisions in real time.

AI-DLC is aimed at complex systems: ones with many architectural trade-offs, scaling and integration needs, or several teams in a large or regulated organisation. The paper says simple apps that need few trade-offs are better built with low-code or no-code tools.

02 · Mental model

The core loop: AI leads, humans decide

The central change in AI-DLC is who starts the conversation. With an assistant, a developer prompts the AI for each task. In AI-DLC, AI takes a high-level intent, breaks it into tasks, proposes options and trade-offs, and then stops to ask. Humans act as approvers. They answer questions, pick between options and confirm decisions at the points that matter.

In practice

Every prompt in the paper's appendix follows the same loop. AI writes its plan to a markdown file with a checkbox per step. It marks any step where it needs your input, and it waits for approval before it starts. Then it works one step at a time and ticks each box as it goes. The plan file is where you review the work and redirect it.

03 · Foundations

Ten principles behind the method

The paper lists ten principles that shape AI-DLC's phases, roles, artefacts and rituals. Knowing them helps when a situation isn't covered by the method and you need to decide what to do.

  1. Reimagine, don't retrofit

    Stand-ups, retros and story points exist because iterations last weeks. AI cycles last hours or days, so the method starts again from first principles. In the paper's words, "we need automobiles and not the faster horse chariots."

  2. Reverse the conversation

    AI starts the conversation and proposes the plan. Humans validate, choose options and confirm decisions, so their time goes on oversight and risk.

  3. Design techniques at the core

    Scrum leaves design practice up to each team. AI-DLC builds it in, with separate flavours for DDD, BDD and TDD. The paper describes the DDD flavour.

  4. Match what AI can do today

    Current AI can't reliably turn intent into working code on its own. Developers keep final responsibility for validation, decisions and oversight.

  5. Built for complex systems

    The method targets systems with many trade-offs, integrations and teams. Simple apps with few trade-offs are better served by low-code tools.

  6. Keep what helps humans and AI align

    User stories stay because they act as a contract between people and AI. The risk register stays so AI plans and code comply with the organisation's risk rules.

  7. Easy to learn from what you know

    A practitioner should be able to start in a day. New terms map onto familiar ones: a Sprint becomes a Bolt, because the cycle is now much shorter.

  8. Fewer, broader roles

    With AI handling decomposition, developers work across front-end, back-end, infrastructure and security. Product owners and developers remain, and the method adds new roles only when it has to.

  9. Few stages, steady flow

    Fewer hand-offs keep work moving. Human checkpoints act like a loss function: they cut wasted downstream work early and stop AI code from setting like "quick-cement".

  10. No fixed workflows

    The method doesn't prescribe a workflow for new builds, refactors or bug fixes. AI proposes a plan for each intent, and humans adjust it level by level.

04 · Core framework

The artefacts

AI-DLC work flows through a small set of artefacts. Each one is saved to the repository, where it becomes context for the next step.

Intent
A high-level statement of what needs to be achieved. It can be a business goal, a feature or a technical outcome such as performance scaling. Everything that AI breaks down starts from an Intent.
Unit
A cohesive, self-contained piece of work derived from an Intent that delivers measurable value. It contains a set of user stories. AI proposes the Units and people refine them. Units are loosely coupled, so they can be built and deployed independently.Similar to an Epic in Scrum or a subdomain in DDD.
Bolt
The smallest iteration: a focused build-and-validate cycle measured in hours or days. A Unit is delivered through one or more Bolts, run in parallel or one after another. AI plans the Bolts, and developers or product owners validate the plan.Similar to a Sprint, but much shorter.
Domain Design
The core business logic of a Unit, modelled without infrastructure. AI uses DDD building blocks: aggregates, value objects, entities, domain events, repositories and factories.
Logical Design
The Domain Design extended to meet non-functional requirements, using architecture patterns such as CQRS or circuit breakers. AI records its choices as Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) for developers to validate.
Code & unit tests
Generated from the Logical Design and mapped to AWS services following well-architected principles. AI runs the unit tests, analyses the results and recommends fixes to the developer.
Deployment Unit
What gets shipped: packaged code (container images, Lambda functions), configuration (Helm charts) and infrastructure (Terraform or CloudFormation stacks). AI generates functional, security and load tests. Humans review the scenarios, then AI runs them and links failures to the code or configuration change that caused them.

05 · Phases & rituals

Three phases, each feeding the next

Development in AI-DLC runs through Inception, Construction and Operations. Each phase gives AI richer context for the next one, so its suggestions get better as the work goes on. Plans, requirements and designs are stored in the project repository, which means work can continue across sessions without losing context.

Phase 1Inception

Inception turns Intents into Units through Mob Elaboration, a group session for elaborating and breaking down requirements.

How to run Mob Elaboration

  • One room, one shared screen, and a facilitator leading the session.
  • AI proposes a first breakdown of the Intent into user stories, acceptance criteria and Units. It aims for loose coupling and high cohesion so Units can be built in parallel.
  • The mob (product owner, developers, QA and other stakeholders) reviews the proposal. They fix parts that are over- or under-engineered and adjust for real-world constraints.

What comes out

  • A PRFAQ
  • User stories
  • Non-functional requirements (NFRs)
  • Risks, matched to the organisation's risk register if one exists
  • Measurement criteria traced back to the business intent
  • Suggested Bolts for building each Unit

According to the paper, Mob Elaboration compresses weeks or months of sequential requirements work into a few hours. It also aligns the team with each other and with the AI.

Phase 2Construction

Construction turns Units into tested, operations-ready Deployment Units. The work moves from Domain Design to Logical Design, then to code and automated tests. At each task AI recommends options such as design patterns, UX choices and tests. Developers validate the output at every step and make the critical calls.

Mob Construction

  • AI-DLC recommends putting all teams in one room, as in Mob Elaboration.
  • Teams exchange integration specifications that come out of the domain model stage.
  • Each team makes its decisions and delivers its Bolts.

Testing loop

  • AI runs functional, security and performance tests and highlights problems.
  • AI proposes fixes, for example tuning query logic for performance.
  • Developers review the findings, approve fixes and rerun the tests.

Phase 3Operations

Operations covers deployment, observability and maintenance. AI packages Deployment Units, and developers approve the configuration and start the rollout. AI then analyses metrics, logs and traces to spot anomalies and predict SLA breaches. It links into your incident runbooks and proposes actions such as scaling, performance tuning or isolating a fault. It carries those actions out once a developer approves. Developers check that each proposal meets SLAs and compliance requirements.

06 · The workflow

Plans inside plans

Every piece of work starts with an intent. It might be a new system, an enhancement to an existing one, a modernisation or a defect fix. AI proposes a Level 1 Plan describing the workflow for that intent, and the team reviews and edits it. AI then breaks each step into Level 2 subtasks, again under review, and continues down until tasks are small enough to execute.

Context memory

All generated artefacts are kept: intents, user stories, domain models, test plans. AI reads them as its memory across the lifecycle. The artefacts are also linked to each other, so you can trace a domain entity back to the user story that required it. That linking helps AI pull the right context at each stage.

07 · AI-DLC in action

Green-field example: a recommendation engine

The paper follows a product owner whose intent is "develop a recommendation engine for cross-selling products". AI recognises this as a new application and proposes a Level 1 Plan. The team corrects it, and then the phases run as follows.

08 · Existing systems

Brown-field: changing a system that already exists

Brown-field work covers new features, NFR improvements, refactoring and defect fixes in an existing application. Inception and Operations are the same as for green-field work. Construction adds one step at the start.

  1. AI reverse-engineers the code into two kinds of model. Static models describe the components, their responsibilities and how they relate. Dynamic models show how the components interact to deliver the most important use cases.
  2. Developers and product managers review the models and correct them.
  3. Construction continues from the domain model as usual, working from the reviewed models.

09 · Vocabulary

New words for familiar ideas

AI-DLC keeps the relationships between Agile concepts and gives new names to the ones whose meaning has changed. Some practices are questioned but not yet replaced. The paper asks, for example, whether velocity should give way to a measure of business value.

10 · Getting started

Try it on your next piece of work

The paper's appendix includes prompts for practising AI-DLC with any capable coding agent. AWS points to Amazon Q Developer project rules and Kiro steering as ways to apply the same instructions consistently across a team.

1. Set up the workspace

Start each session by telling AI where to keep its artefacts. The setup prompt creates this structure:

aidlc-docs/
├── plans/              # every plan, approved before work starts
├── requirements/       # requirements and feature changes
├── story-artifacts/    # user stories
├── design-artifacts/   # architecture and design documents
└── prompts.md          # every prompt used, in order
Setup prompt adapted from Appendix A 
We will work on building an application today. For every front-end and back-end component we will create a project folder. All documents will reside in the aidlc-docs folder.

Throughout our session I'll ask you to plan your work ahead and create an md file for the plan. You may work only after I approve said plan. These plans will always be stored in the aidlc-docs/plans folder.

You will create many types of documents in md format:
- Requirement and feature-change documents go in aidlc-docs/requirements.
- User stories go in aidlc-docs/story-artifacts.
- Architecture and design documents go in aidlc-docs/design-artifacts.
- All prompts, in order, go in aidlc-docs/prompts.md.

Confirm your understanding of this prompt. Create the necessary folders and files if they do not exist already.

2. Use the same shape for every task

Every task prompt in the appendix has the same three parts. A role tells AI which expert to act as. A planning contract makes AI plan first, flag questions and wait for approval. A task says what to produce and where. Keep the contract word for word and change only the role and the task.

Your role: You are an experienced [product manager | software architect | software engineer | cloud architect].

Planning contract: Before you start the task below, plan the work and write your steps in [plan file].md with a checkbox for each step. If any step needs my clarification, add a note to that step so you can get my confirmation. Do not make critical decisions on your own. When the plan is ready, ask for my review and approval. After I approve, carry out the plan one step at a time, and mark each checkbox as done when you finish that step.

Your task: [what to produce, which files to read, where to write the output]

After reviewing and editing the plan, reply with something short such as I approve the plan. Proceed, and mark each checkbox as you complete it.

3. Walk through the phases

These task sections plug into the shape above. Each one reads the previous step's output.

Inception User stories 
Your role: expert product manager, creating well-defined user stories that become the contract for developing the system.
Plan file: user_stories_plan.md

Your task: Build user stories for the high-level requirement described here: [describe the product].
Inception Units 
Your role: experienced software architect.
Plan file: units_plan.md

Your task: Refer to the user stories in mvp_user_stories.md. Group the user stories into multiple units that can be built independently. Each unit contains highly cohesive user stories that can be built by a single team. The units are loosely coupled with each other. For each unit, write its user stories and acceptance criteria in an individual md file in the design/ folder.
Construction Domain (component) model 
Your role: experienced software engineer.
Plan file: design/component_model.md

Your task: Refer to the user stories in design/[unit_name].md. Design the component model to implement all the user stories. The model shall contain all the components, their attributes and behaviours, and how the components interact to implement the user stories. Do not generate any code yet. Write the component model into a separate md file in the design/ folder.
Construction Code generation 
Your role: experienced software engineer.
Plan file: [component]_code_plan.md

Your task: Refer to the component design in [path/to/component].md. Generate a simple and intuitive implementation of the component in that design. Generate the classes in individual files, kept in the [package] directory.
Operations Deployment architecture 
Your role: experienced cloud architect.
Plan file: deployment_plan.md

Your task: Refer to the component design model, the units, the cloud architecture and the back-end code. Then:
- Generate an end-to-end plan for deploying the back end on AWS using [CloudFormation | CDK | Terraform].
- Document all prerequisites for the deployment.

Once I approve the plan:
- Follow the best practice of clean, simple, explainable code.
- Put all output code in the DEPLOYMENT/ folder.
- Validate that the generated code works as intended: create a validation plan and generate a validation report.
- Review the validation report, fix all identified issues, and update the report.

4. Rolling it out across an organisation

AWS positions AI-DLC as a small change from existing Agile methods and suggests two ways to adopt it:

  • Learn by practising. AI-DLC is mostly a set of rituals. Teams learn fastest by running Mob Elaboration and Mob Construction on problems they are already working on, with a guide present, rather than through documents and training. AWS Solution Architects package this approach as a field offering called the AI-DLC Unicorn Gym.
  • Build it into developer tooling. Some organisations run their own orchestration platforms across the SDLC. The paper names Cognizant's FlowSource, Aspire's CodeSpell and HCL's AIForce. If AI-DLC is built into those tools, developers follow it without a separate adoption programme.

You can also contact your AWS account team to discuss fitting AI-DLC to your organisation.
